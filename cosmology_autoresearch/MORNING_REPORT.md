## What looks promising, and what is not

The genuinely promising part is the cross-checking strategy: matched nulls show how often ordinary fits imitate the BAO profile gain; redshift holdouts reveal where a fit succeeds or fails; the Dovekie frozen-shape test now supplies independent, separate-probe prediction information. Its IVS advantage is numerically robust but modest and localized to low redshift—not a discovery. Current primary-source literature also makes the calibration bar clear: the September IVS paper reports that a Bayesian-evidence comparison still favors ΛCDM for its tested interaction scenario, while recent DESI DR2 model-comparison analyses vary materially with the supernova sample and parameterization. Those papers motivate fidelity and robustness checks, not copying their headline significances into this reduced screen. [Yang et al. (2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.05660), [Wang et al. (2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.10567).

The low-effort parameter tweaks are not promising as standalone claims. Curvature's gain is routinely reproduced by the flat null. IVS's gain is common in its own matched null and fails the high-z predictive stress. CPL has the largest exploratory profile gain among these screens, but its tail and domain sensitivity are insufficient to call it a result. A cheap-looking but scientifically useful next step is a Dovekie-only two-parameter background profile with an explicit physical/domain contract. More arbitrary BAO-only curve fitting, further decimal refinement, or trying GPU acceleration on tiny fits would be stupid-simple in the unhelpful sense: they add little information. The last local inventory showed 20 CPUs and an RTX 5090; current demand is near zero because the observed score took 1.55 s and the independent review 5.8 s on one thread. Use more hardware only for a measured, genuinely expensive follow-up.
