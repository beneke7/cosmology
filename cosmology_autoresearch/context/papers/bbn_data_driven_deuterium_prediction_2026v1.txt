                                                               We predict the primordial deuterium abundance using a novel, fully data-driven approach,
                                                            where we use Gaussian process regression to fit experimental nuclear reaction data for
                                                            d(d,n)3 He, d(d,p)t, and d(p,γ)3 He, three reactions to which the primordial deuterium abundance
                                                            is most sensitive. Using the Planck determination of the baryon density, we predict 105 × D/H =
                                                            2.442±0.040 in standard Big Bang Nucleosynthesis, 1.70σ below the Cooke et al. measurement. Our
                                                            result is consistent with predictions relying on first principles calculations of the deuterium burning
arXiv:2604.16600v1 [astro-ph.CO] 17 Apr 2026




                                                            cross sections. With the inferred baryon density from a combined fit to Planck, ACT DR6, and
                                                            SPT-3G D1, this discrepancy worsens to 1.98σ. We validate our approach and confirm that Gaus-
                                                            sian processes make unbiased D/H predictions with appropriately-sized uncertainties. We repeat our
                                                            validation tests for low-degree polynomial fits, a technique used in previous analyses, and find that
                                                            they systematically over-predict D/H. Our results highlight the need for improved measurements of
                                                            the d(d,n)3 He and d(d,p)t S-factors at energies between 0.1 and 0.6 MeV.

Matérn local component. For the local component, we
choose Matérn-1/4 from consideration of the properties
of the Matérn kernel. For fixed correlation length ℓ, cor-
relations between the S-factor at different energies decay
more quickly as ν is lowered. In the ν → 0 limit, k(d) goes
to 0 unless d = 0, and the Matérn kernel becomes noise
added equally to each point. We directly know experi-
mental uncertainties, so we avoid adding extra statistical
noise equally to each data point. Therefore, we cannot
make ν arbitrarily small.
   ν cannot be too large either; setting ν ≫ 1 does not
produce the required small-scale fluctuations. Even for
ν ≥ 0.5, correlations decay slowly enough that fluctua-
tions span energy ranges larger than fluctuations between
data. The local component should only account for fluc-
tuations in the data, and we want to mitigate artifacts
arising from increasing the range of these small-scale fluc-
tuations. The intermediate ν = 1/4 provides the desired
behavior, but we emphasize that our analysis is robust

                                                A.     Comparing Distributions

  We compare the performance of GPs with polynomials to make accurate D/H predictions from fits to mock S-factor
data in the main body. For each mock data realization, we calculate the predicted D/Hpred ± σpred and compare these
to the D/H for the underlying function that generated the data D/Htrue . We do this for 1,000 mocks, and approximate
(D/Hpred − D/Htrue )/σpred distributions as the resulting empirical sample set (see Figure 2).
  A fully unbiased fitting procedure with robust uncertainties would produce a set of (D/Hpred − D/Htrue )/σpred
converging to N (0, 1) for infinite mock realizations. We introduce two quantities to assess the similarity of the
empirical distributions to N (0, 1): the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence [25] and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS)
statistic [26]. We use the quantities as measures of proximity to N (0, 1), enabling a quantitative comparison between
GPs and polynomials across the scenarios shown in Figure 2. Both are non-negative, vanishing only when the compared
distributions are identical. However, they compare different features of the distributions.
  The KL divergence from a reference distribution Q to a distribution P is defined as

Here, the sum runs over histogram bins in Figure 2. P is the empirical distribution and Q is N (0, 1). DKL quantifies
how distinguishable the empirical distribution is from N (0, 1), with larger values indicating greater disagreement. We
find little sensitivity of DKL values to bin width for ≥ 10 bins.
   The KS statistic is defined as
                                                     DKS = sup |F (x) − G(x)| ,                                     (29)

where F (x) is the empirical cumulative distribution function (CDF) and G(x) is the N (0, 1) CDF. The supremum
is taken over all x, so DKS isolates the point at which the two CDFs differ the most. The CDFs F (x) and G(x)
run from 0 to 1, so DKS ∈ [0, 1). Again, larger values indicate greater disagreement, and DKS is zero only when the
two distributions are the same. DKS is therefore most sensitive to localized deviations, such as an excess or lack of
probability in a particular region.
   Since DKL is an integrated quantity weighted by the empirical distribution, it is more sensitive to broad, systematic
differences. On the other hand, DKS highlights the largest local deviation regardless of how the rest of the distribution
compares. Together, they provide a more comprehensive picture of distributional agreement.
   In all scenarios considered in Figure 2, both the KL divergence and KS statistic are consistently smaller for GPs
than for polynomials, with KL divergences a factor of ∼ 5 (10) and KS statistics a factor of ∼ 2 (3) smaller for the
theory (smoothed GP) generating function. Since DKS is bounded between 0 and 1, improvement by an O(1) factor
is significant. Thus, we quantitatively confirm that GPs produce D/H predictions whose distributions are closer to
the expected N (0, 1) behavior of an unbiased procedure with well-calibrated uncertainties.

   Our handling of correlated experimental S-factor data, described in Section III D, could be the underlying cause
for this persistent positive bias in D/H predictions. Ref. [37] found that fitting to correlated data by minimizing a χ2
with nonzero off-diagonal entries in the covariance matrix yields estimators that are biased towards lower values. We
do not minimize a χ2 for GPs, but we do include a covariance matrix with correlations during regression (see Eq. (7)).
A similar effect could produce this slight bias in our analysis; the S-factor reconstructions for GPs are, on average,
slightly lower than the generating function, which is clearly observed in the top-left panel of Figure 2. This slightly
lower d(d,p)t and d(d,n)3 He S-factors that we recover in our MC study leads to less deuterium burning during BBN
and higher D/H, biasing our result toward closer agreement with the Cooke et al. measurement assuming Planck ωb .
